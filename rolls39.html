Generate a BIP-39 seed phrase from entropy you produced yourself. Don't trust, roll.

A hardware wallet can do three jobs: 1) generate a seed, 2) store that seed securely, and 3) sign transactions with it. Storage and signing are what the device is built to do every day. Generating the seed is a setup task that you can do yourself offline and verify.

A seed created inside a device is a number you have to take on faith. You have no way to audit the device's firmware and in 2026 firmware with a broken random number generator led to the loss of Bitcoin from cold storage. Dice have no firmware.

Generating a seed yourself allows you to ensure that a physical source of randomness that cannot be recreated was used. Here you will roll dice, enter the results, and this page will convert them into a BIP-39 seed phrase. Dice are a well-established source of randomness even when they are not perfect.

There are many tools for generating seed phrases with dice. This page is different because it shows the details of each step and guides you to check each step independently. Nothing here asks you to trust the page. Work through the checks and you will know that the entropy you generated is what produced your seed phrase.

what this page walks you through

This page shows you how dice rolls are converted to entropy, how many are needed, and how to compensate for imperfect dice. It shows you how to reproduce the hash that converts the rolls to a number that can be used for a seed phrase using a built-in utility on your own computer. It provides tables, a binary to decimal calculator, and a printable worksheet so you can follow the path from the hash digest to every BIP-39 word in your seed phrase. It even shows you how to calculate your own checksum using your computer's standard utilities. When you are done, you can skip the hardware wallet's own generator and enter the phrase you generated with the confidence that you verified each step and did not trust.

why some vendors tell you not to do this

Several hardware wallet vendors advise against creating a seed phrase outside the device, and the reason is sound. On a networked machine, or one that writes the result to disk, a seed can be copied without you knowing. That risk is severe and it is not hypothetical.

Using an amnesic operating system such as Tails, booted from a USB stick with the network off, keeps nothing after shutdown and writes nothing to disk. This avoids the risks they are warning about. But do not overlook this step. You can use an everyday laptop to practice the verification process, but when it comes time to generate a seed you will fund, do it offline with an amnesic operating system. You can find more instructions at the bottom of the page.

BIP-39 is the seed phrase standard almost every wallet has settled on. A phrase made here restores on most popular hardware wallets such as Trezor, BitBox02, Ledger, Coldcard, Jade, Foundation Passport, and Keystone, among others, and in Electrum by choosing BIP39 under Options.

WARNING: Before you make a seed that will hold money, save this file, disconnect from the network, and open it from disk. The page makes no network calls and stores nothing, and running it offline means you do not have to take that on faith. More instructions are at the bottom of the page.

about the die type setting

A bit is one yes-or-no answer. Doubling the number of faces adds one bit. Two faces is 1 bit, four faces is 2 bits, eight faces is 3 bits.

A d6 die sits between four faces and eight, so it is worth between 2 and 3 bits. The exact relationship is log2(N) where N is the number of faces. For d6 that figure is 2.585. Divide your target by the bits per roll and you have the minimum roll count. 256 bits takes 100 rolls of a fair d6, or 86 of a fair d8. The counter below does the arithmetic for you as you enter rolls.

Because each roll can output a non-integer number of bits, the final string of rolls has to be converted to the form used by BIP-39. There is no BIP standard for this, but using SHA-256 for this conversion is a convenient and sensible choice since it is ubiquitous in the Bitcoin protocol and is a well-known and well-studied one-way function that always outputs 256 bits. See step 3 for more detail.

about the imbalance setting

Min-entropy, in the counter below, is the randomness this tool credits you based on the rolls entered and the assumed imbalance setting. A fair die gets more credit than an imbalanced one that comes up on one face more often than the others.

You cannot measure your dice at your kitchen table. Casino dice have their pips filled flush and are close to fair. Cheap dice with drilled pips run slightly light on the six face, so high numbers should come up a little more often.

The largest careful count of ordinary dice, 315,672 rolls by Zacariah Labby, found high faces about 0.3 percent more often than chance, small enough that it was not statistically distinguishable from fair. The +20% default here is roughly seventy times more generous than that, which is the point: it costs eleven extra rolls and covers a great deal more than pip drilling.

+20% on a d6 means one face turns up 20 percent of the time rather than 16.7 percent of the time.

Fair d6 dice would ask for 100 rolls for 256 bits. +20% asks for 111. Just three extra throws of five covers the bias in a die with a visible defect. The default assumes some imbalance because extra rolling is a trivial cost, but insufficient rolling cannot be undone once the seed holds money.

2  record

Choose real dice or test blob in step 1 to begin.

Turn physical throws into a string of digits. That string is what becomes your seed.

Read the dice left to right, then top to bottom if they cluster. Never sort them. Sorting throws away 38 percent of what you rolled, and this page cannot tell that you did it.

Take a look at step 3 while you enter throws. Every roll you add changes all 64 characters of the digest. One die landing differently produces an entirely different phrase.

rolling well

Roll somewhere private, on a flat surface with a lip, so a die that leaves the table does not leave you guessing. If a die lands tilted or off the surface, throw the whole group again and enter nothing.

With more than a handful in the throw, nudge them into a line without changing which die is which, then read along it. Moving a die is fine. Reordering by value is not.

A throw locks when its last digit is entered, or press Enter to lock a short final throw. Backspace works only in the throw you are on, and a mistyped digit costs no entropy.

3  verify the digest

Appears once you enter your first roll.

This is the one step a computer does for you. Everything after it is arithmetic you can do with a pen, apart from the checksum on the last word. So check this one against a program this page did not write.

Your dice digits go into SHA-256 and come out as the 64-character string below, the digest. Its leading bits become your entropy, and everything downstream follows from it. Run the same hash on your own machine with the command below, then compare. Matching strings mean this page did the computed step honestly.

Hashing a single digit "1" gives a 256-bit digest and a seed phrase that looks plausibly random. There is no way to tell how much entropy went into a phrase by looking at it. The only way to know is to verify the source of randomness used to generate it. That is why this page exists.

what SHA-256 is doing here

SHA-256 turns an input of any length into exactly 256 bits. The same input gives the same output on every machine, which is what lets you check it. It does not run backwards, so the digest tells nobody what you rolled.

It is not a source of randomness. Every bit of randomness in your seed came off the dice. The hash packs what you rolled into the shape BIP-39 needs; it does not add randomness.

SHA-256 needs the input to be exact. One extra space or a trailing newline gives a completely different digest. This is why the command below uses printf rather than echo to ensure there are no spurious characters being hashed.

4  digest to words

Appears once you confirm the digest, or choose to skip that check.

Your seed phrase is built by grouping the bits in the string from step 3. This section walks through that grouping so you can check it on paper.

bits, bytes, hex, decimal, and BIP-39 words

A bit is one digit that can be a 1 or 0. Eight bits make a byte. Hex (base-16) is a more compact representation of the same bits: one character [0-9,a-f] represents four bits; two characters represent a byte. The string from step 3 is 64 hex characters, 32 bytes, 256 bits. Same number, three ways of writing it.

BIP-39 does not read the bits in sets of four or eight. Instead, it takes them eleven at a time, because eleven bits is exactly enough to count from 0 to 2047 and the wordlist holds 2048 words. For each block of 11 bits, convert it to decimal, look up that number in the list, and you have a BIP-39 seed word. Repeat until the bits run out.

To read eleven bits as a number, the first bit is worth 1024, the second 512, and so on down to the last, which is worth 1. Add up the bits that are set. The bit calculator at the bottom of this section does that arithmetic as you flip bits.

checking this against another tool

Any BIP-39 tool with a raw entropy field will turn this same entropy into these same words. Copy the bright hex above and paste it in, offline, and you get a second program agreeing with this one.

Paste only the bright characters. At 24 words that is all 64. At 12 words it is the first 32, because the dimmed remainder is discarded rather than part of your entropy.

This confirms two programs agree. Using the printed wordlist and worksheet below, you can confirm the answer without trusting either one.

The last few bits are a checksum. They are not random and they are not secret. They exist so a wallet can tell you whether you mistyped a word. You cannot compute them with a pen, and they do not affect how hard your seed is to guess.

confirm the import

After entering the phrase, compare the first receive address your device shows against one derived independently. On Tails, Electrum is already installed: restore from your phrase, choosing BIP39 under Options, with path m/84'/0'/0'. Matching addresses mean the device is deriving from the seed you gave it. Compare an address rather than an xpub.

If they do not match, the usual cause is a setting rather than a fault: a different address type, a different derivation path, a passphrase set on one side only, or a different account number. Check those before assuming the device is wrong.

Ian Coleman's bip39-standalone.html also does this offline if Electrum is unavailable. The source and its releases are at github.com/iancoleman/bip39. Paste the phrase into the BIP39 Phrase field. Do not enter your dice rolls there: Coleman's dice mode rewrites every 6 as a 0 before hashing, so the same dice give a different phrase.

How this is meant to be used. Generate the phrase here on an offline machine, write it down, then import it into your hardware wallet instead of letting the device create one.

Running this offline. Save the file, disconnect, open it from disk. On Tails, place it in a folder Tor Browser can read: the Tor Browser folder in Home or in Persistent Storage on older releases, any Home or Persistent Storage folder from 6.14.1 onward. Files under /usr or /etc will not open.

What this does not do. No BIP-32, no BIP-44, no addresses. Verifying a phrase needs SHA-256 and the BIP-39 wordlist. Verifying an address needs elliptic curve code, and your hardware wallet already shows addresses after import. Keeping that out is what makes this file short enough to read.
