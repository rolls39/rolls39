Generate a BIP-39 seed phrase from entropy you produced yourself, with the means to check every step. Don't trust, roll.

A hardware wallet does three jobs. It creates a seed, it stores that seed, and it signs transactions with it. Storage and signing are what the device is built for. Creating the seed is the one job you can do yourself and check afterward.

A seed created inside a device is a number you have to take on faith. Firmware has shipped with a broken random number generator before. Dice have no firmware.

Here you will roll dice, this page will convert your rolls into a BIP-39 seed phrase, and you will confirm the result two ways: a second program on your own computer for the one computed step, and a printed wordlist for every word. Then you type the phrase into your wallet and skip its own generator.

Here you will roll dice and this page will convert your rolls into a BIP-39 seed phrase. Simple mode shows three values: your rolls, the SHA-256 of your rolls, and the phrase. Untick the box below to see how each of those is checked against a second program and a printed wordlist.

the case for letting the device generate

Some vendors advise the opposite of this page. Blockstream's setup guide for Jade tells users that restoring a phrase created on a phone or computer gives hardware signing without changing the security of the phrase itself, and recommends creating a new wallet on the device instead.

That advice fits the machine it has in mind. A phone or an everyday laptop is networked, keeps files, and runs software you did not choose. A seed made there is exposed to all of it.

It does not follow for a machine chosen for the job. An amnesic operating system with the network off, writing nothing to disk, is also a computer, and the two carry different risk. Collapsing them into one category is what makes the advice look universal.

What is left is a trade, and it is worth stating plainly. Generating on the device takes the machine out of the picture and leaves you holding a number you cannot check. Generating here makes you responsible for the machine and lets you check every step of the conversion. Neither removes risk. One of them can be audited.

Blockstream also publishes a dice guide for Jade, which calculates the final word of a phrase you rolled yourself. So the disagreement is narrower than it looks. Both approaches accept dice as the source. The open question is where the arithmetic runs, and whether you can see it.

BIP-39 is the seed phrase standard almost every wallet has settled on. A phrase made here restores on Trezor, BitBox, Ledger, Coldcard, Jade, Foundation Passport, Keystone and SeedSigner, among others, and in Electrum by choosing BIP39 under Options.

Every step on this page can be checked independently.

Before you start: save this file, disconnect from the network, and open the file from disk. The page makes no network calls and stores nothing. Running it offline means you do not have to take that on faith.

1  setup

Choose how you will use the tool, and what you are aiming for.

Changing the die or the dice per throw clears any rolls already entered, and asks first. Digits rolled on one die are not rolls of another, and a box is meant to be one physical throw. Phrase length and imbalance can be changed at any time, because they re-credit the same rolls rather than reinterpreting them.

Phrase length can be 24 words or 12. Both are standard and every wallet supports both, so pick one and stay with it. Dice per throw is however many dice you have in your hand, and it only changes how the boxes are grouped.

about the die type setting

A fair die with n faces is worth log2(n) bits per roll, which is the figure beside each entry. More faces means fewer rolls: 100 rolls of a d6 for 256 bits, 86 of a d8.

The list stops at d8 so that one roll is always one character. A d16 face needs two digits, which would need either a separator or zero padding, and 03 for a 3. Count the characters and the appeal goes: 256 bits takes 100 characters on a d6, 86 on a d8, and 128 on a d16. Fewer throws, more typing, and a string that no longer reads straight off the dice.

Keeping every face to one character also keeps 0 off the keyboard, so a stray 0 is a rejected keystroke on any setting rather than a legal face on some.

The d6 convention is the only one other tools share. Coldcard, rolls.py and bitcoiner.guide all hash the digits 1 to 6 as typed. A d4 or d8 phrase can be reproduced from this file and from a shell, and not from another dice tool.

Whatever die you pick, read the dice in position and never sort them. That rule does not change with the face count.

about the imbalance setting

This sets how much randomness the tool credits you per roll, which sets how many rolls it asks for. Casino dice are close to fair. Cheap dice with drilled pips run light on the six face, because each drilled pip takes away a little material. You cannot measure this at your kitchen table.

Imbalance is stated as how much more likely the most likely face is than a fair face. At +20% on a d6, one face comes up 20 percent of the time rather than 16.7. The cost in bits is log2(1 + 0.20), which is 0.263 bits per roll, and that cost is the same for every die on the list. That is why one setting serves all of them and why the figures beside it are fixed.

Assuming fair dice asks for 100 rolls of a d6 to reach 256 bits. Assuming +20% asks for 111, which is three extra throws of five dice. Rolling long costs you a minute. Rolling short costs you margin you cannot get back once the seed is funded. The default assumes some imbalance for that reason.

+20% is far more pessimistic than measured dice. It is not a model of your die. It covers what a per-roll figure cannot: a chipped or loaded die, a surface that favours an orientation, throws that barely tumble, and a reader who resolves an ambiguous position the same way every time.

Min-entropy in the counter below is the randomness this tool is willing to credit you, using the worst case for the imbalance you picked rather than the average.

2  record

Choose real dice or test blob in step 1 to begin.

Turn physical throws into a string of digits. That string, and nothing else about the throw, is what becomes your seed.

Read the dice left to right, then top to bottom if they cluster. Never sort them.

rolling well

Roll somewhere private, on a flat surface with a lip, so a die that leaves the table does not leave you guessing. If a die lands tilted or off the surface, throw the whole group again and enter nothing.

Read the dice in the same order every time. Do not arrange them and do not put the low ones first.

With more than a handful in the throw, nudge them into a line without changing which die is which, then read along it. Moving a die is fine. Reordering by value is not.

A throw locks when its last digit lands, or press Enter to lock a short final throw. Backspace works only in the throw you are on, and a mistyped digit costs no entropy.

why order matters

Read in position, five fair d6 give 2.585 bits each. Sorting them by value keeps only which numbers came up and discards which die showed what, which leaves about 1.6 bits per die. That is 38 percent of the throw gone. The figures differ with other dice and the rule does not: read in position, never sort. This page sees only the digits you type, so it cannot tell that you sorted.

3  verify the digest

Appears once you enter your first roll.

This is the one step a computer does for you. Everything after it is arithmetic you can do with a pen, so check this one against a program this page did not write.

Your dice digits go into SHA-256 and come out as the string below. Its leading bits become your entropy, and everything downstream follows from it. Run the same hash on your own machine with the command below, then compare. Matching strings mean this page did the computed step honestly.

what SHA-256 is doing here

SHA-256 takes an input of any length and produces exactly 256 bits. The same input gives the same output every time on any machine, which is what makes the check in this step possible. Change one digit of your rolls and the whole output changes with no resemblance to the old one. There is no known way to run it backwards, so the output tells nobody what you rolled.

SHA-256 is not a source of randomness. Every bit of randomness in your seed came off the dice. The hash takes what you rolled and packs it into a fixed 256-bit number, which is the shape BIP-39 needs.

What it does need is the exact input. One extra space or a trailing newline produces a completely different digest, which is why the command below uses printf instead of echo.

Your digits are hashed exactly as rolled, which is the convention Coldcard, rolls.py and bitcoiner.guide all use for a d6. Ian Coleman's dice mode rewrites every 6 as a 0 before hashing, so the same dice give a different phrase there.

4  digest to words

Appears once you confirm the digest, or choose to skip that check.

Your seed phrase is built by grouping the bits in the string from step 3. This section walks through that grouping so you can repeat it on paper.

bits, bytes and hex

A bit is one 1 or 0. Eight bits make a byte. Writing 256 bits out longhand takes 256 characters, so the same value gets written in hex, where each character stands for exactly four bits. The string from step 3 is 64 hex characters, 32 bytes, 256 bits. Same number, three ways of writing it.

The table below lists all sixteen hex characters and the four bits each one means. Tap any hex character in the string to light up its four bits underneath.

BIP-39 does not read the bits in fours or eights. It takes them eleven at a time, because eleven bits is exactly enough to count from 0 to 2047 and the wordlist holds 2048 words. Look up that number in the list and you have a word. Repeat until the bits run out.

That is why the binary row has two kinds of grouping that disagree. The spaces fall every eight bits so you can check the row against the hex above it. The colour changes every eleven bits so you can match each group to a row in the table below.

The last few bits are a checksum. They are not random and they are not secret. They exist so a wallet can tell you when you have mistyped a word. You cannot compute them with a pen, and they do not affect how hard your seed is to guess.

#11 bitsvalueword

Value is the eleven bits added up, 1024 down to 1 from left to right. BIP-39 counts from 0, so a printed list reads one higher: maid at 1072 is line 1073. The bit calculator below does that addition. The marked bits in the table are the checksum. Tap them to jump to the block that confirms them.

confirm the import

After entering the phrase, compare the first receive address the device shows against one derived independently. On Tails, Electrum is already installed: restore from your phrase, choosing BIP39 under Options, with path m/84'/0'/0'. Matching addresses mean the device is deriving from the seed you gave it. Compare an address rather than an xpub, because the same key prints as xpub, ypub or zpub depending on script type and two correct tools can show different strings.

If they do not match, check these before assuming the device is at fault: script type (84' native segwit, 44' legacy, 49' wrapped, 86' taproot), the derivation path, whether a passphrase is set on either side, and the account index. A mismatch is far more often one of those. Loading the phrase into a second hardware wallet works too, if you have one.

Ian Coleman's bip39-standalone.html also does this offline if Electrum is unavailable. Paste the phrase into the BIP39 Phrase field. Do not enter your dice rolls there: Coleman's dice mode rewrites every 6 as a 0 before hashing, so the same dice give a different phrase.

what these checks mean

pass means the file reproduced a known correct answer. FAIL means it did not, and the file should not be used for a seed you intend to fund. unavailable appears only for the WebCrypto line and is not a failure: the browser did not expose its own SHA-256, usually because the page was opened from an opaque origin, so the built-in implementation ran alone with one fewer corroboration.

The checks are: SHA-256 against the standard abc and empty-string vectors, the BIP-39 wordlist against its published hash, three BIP-39 entropy-to-mnemonic vectors from trezor/python-mnemonic, and the built-in SHA-256 against the browser's own. All of them run inside this file, so they cannot detect a browser that is lying. Only hashing your rolls on a second machine can do that.

How this is meant to be used. Generate your seed phrase here on an offline machine, record it safely, then import it into your hardware wallet and skip that device's own generation path entirely. A hardware wallet is good at storing a seed and signing transactions, and it does not need to create one. Trying instead to reproduce a phrase the device generated means trusting a convention in firmware you cannot inspect, which adds an unknown rather than removing one.

Running this offline. Save the file, disconnect, open it from disk. On Tails, place it in a folder Tor Browser can read: the Tor Browser folder in Home or in Persistent Storage on older releases, any Home or Persistent Storage folder from 6.14.1 onward. Files under /usr or /etc will not open.

What this does not do. No BIP-32, no BIP-44, no addresses. Verifying a phrase needs SHA-256 and the BIP-39 wordlist. Verifying an address needs elliptic curve code, and your hardware wallet already shows addresses after import. Keeping that out is what makes this file short enough to read.
